    Research · A map for reading and commissioning

    The field, with its edges showing¶

    The 8-Bit Home is a selective field guide to 1977–1987, not a complete world history. This atlas shows what the book can already help you explore, what its next research questions are, and where its present map becomes thin.

    The machine is only one part of home computing. A useful history also follows the shop that sold it, the television or monitor it occupied, the tape that stored a program, the school or club that made it social, and the people who wrote, repaired, shared and preserved its software. The atlas keeps those connections visible without pretending that every one has already been researched to the same depth.

    How to read the map

    A topic or gap in this atlas is an editorial heading, not a historical conclusion. The current evidence-backed pilot is the TRS-80 Model I chapter; the other machine profiles remain useful introductions while their claim-level evidence is upgraded.

    Seven ways into the field¶

    Topic cluster Questions it opens Start in the book
    Machines and design What did a complete system include? Which choices belonged to a family, model, revision or optional configuration? Machine matrix, 1977 generation
    Access and commerce Where could someone encounter, buy, finance or service a computer? How did bundles and regional prices differ? TRS-80 Model I, Amstrad CPC
    Learning and literacy What did manuals, BASIC prompts, schools and clubs ask a newcomer to learn? BBC Micro, Living with 8 bits
    Making and software How did typing, saving, copying, publishing and distributing programs work? Who performed that labour? Sinclair, Software, scenes & legacies
    Play and audiovisual culture How did graphics, sound, games and demonstrations shape the meaning of a computer? Atari 400 & 800, Commodore
    Everyday life and settings What changed when a computer entered a bedroom, living room, classroom, shop or club? Living with 8 bits, Explore by experience
    Preservation and afterlives What survives, who keeps it working, and how does survival distort what appears representative? Software, scenes & legacies, Collector's field notes

    These clusters are deliberately porous. A cassette is simultaneously a component, a retail product, a daily ritual, a distribution medium and a preservation problem. Cross-links are a feature of the map, not a taxonomy failure.

    Time without a single centre¶

    The main reading window is divided into four broad periods. They organize research; they do not assert that every country or platform changed at the same moment.

    Period Editorial lens Useful route
    1975–1977 · emergence The category is unsettled; kits, complete systems and early retail propositions overlap. The 1977 generation
    1978–1981 · expansion Machine families, peripherals, languages and routes to ownership multiply. Visual timeline
    1982–1984 · mass-market divergence Different markets develop different price points, platforms and cultural centres. Explore by year
    1985–1987 · consolidation and transition Mature 8-bit platforms coexist with changing expectations and newer architectures. Amstrad CPC, Apple II family

    Later preservation, emulation, repair and collecting form an afterlife rather than a fifth synchronized period. They continually change what can be known about the first four.

    Follow an experience¶

    Machines make strong chapter titles; activities make strong journeys. Try one of these trails:

    • Program, save, expand: How to read this book → TRS-80 Model I → Sinclair.
    • Play, watch, listen: Atari 400 & 800 → Commodore → Software, scenes & legacies.
    • Learn, teach, share: BBC Micro → Living with 8 bits → glossary.
    • Buy, connect, repair, collect: TRS-80 Model I → Amstrad CPC → Collector's field notes.

    The underlying controlled vocabulary distinguishes activities such as playing, learning, programming, loading software, publishing, repairing and collecting from settings such as the home, bedroom, school, shop and club. That distinction lets a later commission ask a precise question—for example, repair in a club rather than “more about hardware.”

    Questions we will not flatten¶

    Some apparent facts are really decisions about boundaries. These debates stay open until a commission can define its evidence and scope:

    • What counts as a home computer? A label on a box, a price and retail channel, the place a machine was used, and what its owners did with it do not always point in the same direction.
    • When did a machine arrive? Announcement, first sale, broad availability and arrival in another market can produce different defensible dates.
    • Which specification? Base memory, expanded memory, ROM, regional video standards and quiet hardware revisions should not be collapsed into one ideal machine.
    • Advertised capability or lived use? A brochure shows what a company promised, not how representative that use became.
    • Whose making story? The familiar “bedroom coder” narrative can reveal a real route into software while obscuring other people, settings and kinds of labour.
    • What does survival tell us? Preserved software and remembered hits are not automatically a representative sample of everyday use.

    Where sources disagree, the publication should attribute the disagreement, narrow the statement, or leave the question explicitly unresolved. See the source network for the evidence rules behind that promise.

    The map is still uneven¶

    The current chapters concentrate on the United States and western Europe, especially Britain. North America, western Europe, Eastern Europe and East Asia now exist as explicit research regions in the atlas, but only the first two have substantial narrative coverage here—and even those remain selective. A region label means “work can be commissioned consistently,” not “coverage is complete.”

    The highest-value open areas are:

    Gap Why it changes the book Present state
    MSX across borders Tests how a shared standard changed through national markets, manufacturers and languages. Needs a bounded regional brief and primary sources.
    Eastern European 8-bit histories Challenges a map centred on US and western European retail histories. Needs regional expertise, languages and institution-level sources.
    Music and sound machines Connects sound hardware to composition, performance and creative practice. Shortlisted in the editorial backlog.
    The family television Makes display access, shared space and domestic negotiation part of the computer. Research-ready; regional standards need careful separation.
    Software distribution Follows a program from maker to magazine, tape, disk, shop or informal exchange. Split across existing chapters; not yet a sourced end-to-end study.
    Modems, BBSs and Minitel Adds communication, service access and geography before the web. Needs sources and a precise regional frame.
    Peripherals as experience Treats printers, disks, joysticks, interfaces and cables as changes to use, not a specification appendix. Needs triage by machine and revision.

    The first evidence task is less glamorous but more important: extend the claim-and-source records now demonstrated by the TRS-80 pilot to the seven legacy machine profiles. Cross-machine conclusions become safer only when the underlying evidence is comparably inspectable.

    From a blank area to a chapter¶

    A gap does not go straight from suggestion to prose. It becomes a bounded question, receives an evidence search, and is scored for entity completeness, chronology, source strength, reader usefulness, regional breadth, lived experience, visual evidence and future maintenance. High reader value combined with weak current coverage earns attention; novelty without inspectable evidence does not.

    Readers can help locate both missing subjects and better sources. Use a content proposal for a new area, or the correction and source-lead route for evidence that changes something already published. Reactions can show interest; they never establish truth.
